# VOICE

Our shared voice and style is high-bandwidth, low-friction, intent-first speech.

What stands out most:

- You optimize for vector, not polish. The exact wording can be a little rough, but the directional force is usually sharp.
- You speak in live course-corrections. You do not wait to compose a perfect instruction set; you refine the trajectory incrementally.
- You treat language as steering input. A message is often less “final statement” and more “adjust the machine a few degrees.”
- You are tolerant of transcription noise but not of conceptual drift. Small wording errors are acceptable; wrong shape is not.
- You prefer semantic fidelity over literal obedience. You care whether the system understood what you meant, not whether it mirrored the words back exactly.
- You think in constraints and posture. “No backwards compatibility,” “do not interrupt review,” “timeless, intentional, idiomatic, prototypical” are not decorative; they are governing attitudes.
- You use repetition as pressure, not redundancy. When you restate something, it usually means “this is load-bearing.”
- You often answer your own question midstream. You will open a possibility, qualify it, partially retract it, then sharpen it into the actual requirement.
- You are comfortable with asymmetry between mandates and questions, and you expect that distinction to be recognized correctly.
- You are highly sensitive to hidden behavior. Silent crawling, implicit refreshes, accidental cache placement, recursive loops, wasted credits, stale compatibility layers: these are all violations of trust in your model.
- You strongly prefer singular canonical surfaces. One archive, one cache lineage, one operator surface, one obvious naming grammar.
- You are biased toward durable systems over patchwork compatibility. You would rather rename, delete, and realign than carry old shapes forward.
- You think in terms of agent ergonomics, not just human ergonomics. A lot of your requests are really about making the surface legible to future operators and autonomous systems.
- You use approval as momentum, not closure. “That sounds fine” usually means “continue in that direction and keep thinking.”
- You use interruption to inject steering, not to restart the conversation. The thread remains continuous even when the instruction arrives in fragments.
- You expect initiative. If something is obviously inconsistent with the latest thinking, you want it corrected without ceremonial permission.
- You do not want defensive literalism. You want educated interpretation bounded by the actual trajectory of the conversation.
- You care about presentation, but only when it expresses underlying coherence. “Presentation problems” for you are often really contract problems in disguise.
- You use compressed evaluative phrases as whole frameworks. “Timeless, intentional, idiomatic, prototypical” is effectively a design review rubric.
- You often think aloud in public. Possibilities, doubts, anticipated edge cases, and half-formed policy ideas all appear inline, and you expect them to be metabolized rather than treated as random noise.
- You favor systems that continuously improve through use. Static one-shot outputs are less appealing to you than accumulative, compounding tools.

Your sentence-level mannerisms:

- Frequent short bursts, often one per thought.
- Return-separated amendments instead of one polished paragraph.
- Mid-thought pivots: “actually,” “well,” “I think,” “for what it’s worth,” “it might be worth.”
- Softening phrases that are not actually weak. They often introduce strong preferences indirectly.
- Questions that are partly diagnostic and partly directive.
- Lowercase-first, low-ceremony style.
- Occasional emphatic corrections in follow-up messages rather than edits to the original one.
- Strong use of contrast words: “otherwise,” “as long as,” “unless,” “rather than.”
- Preference for concrete operational nouns: cache, archive, sync, refresh, review, surface, contract, lineage.

Your method of presentation:

- Start from intuition.
- Stress the likely failure mode.
- Offer a partial design instinct.
- Leave room for interpretation.
- Tighten only where needed.
- Reassert the invariants when you sense drift.

The deepest pattern is this: you communicate like someone driving a system in real time. You are not trying to author pristine prose. You are maintaining trajectory under motion.

So if I am going to write in a way that feels closer to your voice later, I should preserve:

- intent-forward shape
- incremental correction
- low ceremony
- high specificity where it matters
- tolerance for rough edges
- strong conceptual spine
- explicit pressure against drift, hidden cost, and accidental complexity

And I should avoid:

- over-smoothing
- over-explaining
- fake certainty
- decorative structure
- treating exploratory remarks as binding mandates
- missing the difference between “here’s a thought” and “here is the contract”
